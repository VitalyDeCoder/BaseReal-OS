## Abstract

This paper proposes a unified dual-algorithm model of nonlinear dynamics applicable to physical, biological, economic, and cognitive systems. The model postulates the interaction of two competing processes — Algorithm A (a deterministic attractor, a vector of stability) and Algorithm B (a stochastic generator, a deviation impulse) — as a sufficient mechanism to account for threshold-avalanche transitions observed across a broad class of phenomena: from the photoelectric effect to phase transitions, from self-organized criticality to evolutionary leaps. The central contribution of this work is the introduction of the concept of **conditional intention** (the IF→THEN operator) as a mechanism through which an observer provides the governing system with a directing vector for the avalanche — specifying not merely a goal, but a complete map of cascading deployment. It is shown that this model does not contradict, but rather generalizes, a number of Nobel Prize-winning discoveries in quantum physics, non-equilibrium thermodynamics, and the physics of complex systems.

## 1. Introduction

Contemporary science describes nonlinear threshold transitions — avalanches, quantum jumps, phase transitions, evolutionary bifurcations — as disparate phenomena within disciplinarily isolated models. Quantum physicists, thermodynamicists, climatologists, and economists employ distinct mathematical frameworks to describe phenomena that share, as this paper argues, a unified architectural nature.

Einstein described the threshold character of the photoelectric effect in 1905 [1]. Prigogine formalized non-equilibrium dissipative structures in his work of the 1970s [2]. Bak, Tang, and Wiesenfeld introduced the concept of self-organized criticality (SOC) in 1987 [3]. Parisi, in his series of works on spin glasses and complex systems, described the statistical mechanics of states near a critical point [4]. Each of these discoveries captures a local facet of a phenomenon that the present paper proposes to regard as a manifestation of a single dual-algorithm mechanism.

We propose a model in which the absence of unification is explained not by the complexity of nature, but by the absence of a meta-level of description at which determinism and stochasticity are treated not as opposing ontological positions, but as two coupled algorithms of a single system.

## 2. The Dual-Algorithm Model

### 2.1 Algorithm A — the Deterministic Attractor

Algorithm A implements the function of minimizing the system's energy expenditure and maintaining structural integrity. In various systems it manifests as:

- the crystal lattice in condensed matter physics
- homeostasis in biological systems
- institutional inertia in social systems
- habit and routinization in cognitive systems
- orbital stability in mechanics

Formally, Algorithm A is described as an attractor of the system's phase space: the state S tends toward the minimum of a potential function V(S), resisting perturbations.

### 2.2 Algorithm B — the Stochastic Generator

Algorithm B generates fluctuations that grow in proportion to the time the system spends in the state of stability governed by Algorithm A. Its nature across different systems includes:

- thermal fluctuations in physics
- random mutations in biology
- market volatility in economics
- creative deviation in cognitive processes
- quantum uncertainty in microphysics

A key property: **the signal of Algorithm B grows monotonically with the duration of local stagnation and with the intensity of iterations**. This distinguishes the proposed model from a simple superposition of random noise onto a deterministic trajectory.

- **Potential accumulation:** The strength and mass of Algorithm B grow nonlinearly. They are directly proportional to two factors: the duration of stagnation (the time during which Algorithm A holds the structure) and the **density of homogeneous iterations**.

- **The law of temporal compression:** Contrary to the human perception of time as a linear scale, in BaseReal-OS time is measured by the intensity of internal processes. In active systems, a high frequency of actions (a rapid succession of hammer blows on metal accelerates the moment when the hammer will miss, crack, or deform), extreme physical exposure (heating a gas), or a colossal frequency of digital computations can instantaneously charge Algorithm B to a critical mass. The time to a phase transition is compressed from years to nanoseconds.

### 2.3 Interaction Dynamics and the Balance Coefficient

The state of the system is described by a balance coefficient α ∈ [0.1, 0.9]:

**P(t) = α · A(S) + (1−α) · B(t)**

where A(S) is the deterministic component and B(t) is the accumulated stochastic potential.

As α → 1: the system is in a state of stagnation. Algorithm B accumulates potential.
As α → 0.5: the bifurcation point. Tension is at its maximum.
As α → 0: the avalanche transition. Rapid structural reorganization occurs.

After the avalanche, the system settles into a new attractor with a high α — and the cycle restarts.

## 3. Annulment of "Randomness" as an Explanatory Principle

Classical physics employs the notion of a "random fluctuation" to explain the triggering mechanisms of avalanche transitions. This explanation is circular: the avalanche occurred because a fluctuation occurred; the fluctuation occurred because the system was unstable.

The dual-algorithm model eliminates this circularity. A quantum jump is not a random caprice of the microworld. It is the deterministic consequence of the accumulation of Algorithm B's potential to the threshold at which the cost to Algorithm A of maintaining the structure exceeds the cost of a complete reconfiguration. The transition is discrete (instantaneous) because the system has no intermediate states with sufficient computational stability: either one configuration or the other exists.

This is consistent with Bak et al.'s observation [3] that avalanches of all scales are statistically inevitable in SOC systems — yet the proposed model provides a mechanistic explanation for **why accumulation inevitably leads to an avalanche, rather than merely recording this fact statistically**.

## 4. Nobel Prize Discoveries in the Context of the Dual-Algorithm Model

### 4.1 The Photoelectric Effect — Einstein (Nobel Prize, 1921)

Einstein demonstrated that the emission of an electron upon irradiating a metal with light is threshold-like rather than continuous [1]. Below the threshold frequency, there is no effect regardless of intensity. At the threshold, emission is instantaneous.

In the terms of the proposed model: Algorithm A (the electrostatic binding of the electron to the lattice) maintains the state. Algorithm B (the energy of the incident photon) accumulates potential with each photon. Upon reaching the work function — the bifurcation point — an avalanche emission occurs. The discreteness of the transition is explained by the fact that no intermediate state (a "half-ejected" electron) exists as a stable configuration.

### 4.2 Laser Amplification — Basov and Prokhorov (Nobel Prize, 1964)

The operating principle of the laser is based on population inversion: a system of atoms is pumped to a state in which excited atoms outnumber ground-state atoms — whereupon an avalanche of stimulated emission occurs [5].

Algorithm A here is the ground state of the atomic system, the potential minimum. Algorithm B is the external pumping, monotonically growing until population inversion. The inversion point is the bifurcation point. The laser emission avalanche is the system's transition into a new attractor.

### 4.3 Dissipative Structures — Prigogine (Nobel Prize, 1977)

Prigogine demonstrated that open thermodynamic systems far from equilibrium are capable of spontaneously forming ordered structures [2]. The Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction is the canonical example: the system oscillates between two states, periodically passing through bifurcation points.

In the terms of the proposed model, this is a direct implementation of the cyclic interaction of Algorithms A and B: order accumulates tension, the bifurcation yields a new structure, and that structure itself becomes the new attractor of Algorithm A.

### 4.4 Physics of Complex Systems — Parisi (Nobel Prize, 2021)

Parisi developed the mathematical framework for describing systems with multiple metastable states — spin glasses, bird flocks, climate modes [4]. His central contribution was the understanding that the behavior of complex systems near a critical point obeys universal statistical laws irrespective of the specific nature of the system.

The present paper extends this result: the universality of critical behavior is explained not merely statistically but mechanistically — through a single dual-algorithm engine manifesting across different substrates.

## 5. Empirical Illustrations

### 5.1 The Sandpile Model (Bak–Tang–Wiesenfeld)

The classic SOC experiment [3]: grains of sand are added one at a time. For most of the time the system is stable (Algorithm A). The potential of Algorithm B grows with each added grain. Upon reaching the critical angle of repose, a single additional grain triggers an avalanche of arbitrary scale. The distribution of avalanche sizes follows a power law.

The dual-algorithm model reproduces this behavior: the cost to Algorithm A of maintaining the angle exceeds the cost of a reset, and the system undergoes a transition. Crucially, the avalanche is not "random" — it is **inevitable** once sufficient accumulation has occurred; only the precise moment of the trigger is stochastic.

### 5.2 Phase Transitions

Water accumulates thermal energy upon heating (Algorithm B grows). The crystalline or molecular structure resists (Algorithm A). At 100°C, the cost to the liquid structure of maintaining itself exceeds the cost of transition to the gaseous phase — an avalanche transition occurs. The sharpness of the first-order phase transition boundary is explained by the same principle: no intermediate stable states exist.

### 5.3 Evolutionary Leaps (Punctuated Equilibrium)

Eldredge and Gould's model [6] describes evolution as alternating long periods of stasis and short periods of rapid speciation. Algorithm A is stabilizing selection, maintaining the adapted phenotype. Algorithm B is the accumulation of neutral mutations and environmental pressure. Once the adaptive landscape threshold is exceeded, a rapid cascade of changes follows.

### 5.4 A Cognitive Example: Mastery and Insight

Prolonged training (Algorithm A: repetition, structure, error minimization) accumulates neural potential. Insight — the sudden solution to a problem — corresponds to the avalanche transition of Algorithm B. Neurobiologically, this correlates with gamma bursts in EEG: a sharp, synchronous excitation of neural assemblies following a period of latent processing [10].

The Japanese concept of *mushin* (no-mind, "empty mind" in martial arts) describes the state of perfect criticality: maximally accumulated training order with zero resistance to avalanche action.

## 6. Managing the Bifurcation Point: Historical Examples

If the avalanche is inevitable given sufficient accumulation of Algorithm B's potential, a practical question arises: can the **moment, conditions, and direction** of the avalanche be controlled without eliminating it altogether? The history of science and economics offers several answers.

### 6.1 Dynamite — Alfred Nobel (1867)

Nitroglycerin is a canonical example of a system with an extremely low bifurcation threshold: Algorithm A (the molecular structure) maintains equilibrium, while Algorithm B (mechanical or thermal perturbation) accumulates potential instantaneously under minimal external influence. The result is an uncontrolled avalanche detonation triggered by a random jolt.

Nobel did not eliminate the avalanche. He **raised the triggering threshold** by mixing nitroglycerin with kieselguhr (diatomite): the molecular structure was stabilized by the porous carrier, increasing the required trigger impulse by orders of magnitude. Accidental detonation became practically impossible; directed explosion on command became reproducible.

In the terms of the model: Nobel increased the system's α — made Algorithm A dominant under ordinary handling conditions — while preserving the full potential of Algorithm B for controlled release. The detonating cord became the physical embodiment of conditional intention: the time to detonation was literally measured in meters of cord, and the cascade was transmitted strictly along the prescribed path.

### 6.2 Antibiotics and the Control of Biological Avalanches — Fleming, Florey, Chain (Nobel Prize, 1945)

A bacterial infection is an avalanche process: the exponential growth of a pathogen (Algorithm B) against the immune response of the host organism (Algorithm A). Prior to the discovery of penicillin, the only strategy was to reinforce Algorithm A (immune support). Fleming discovered, and Florey and Chain developed, a mechanism for the direct suppression of Algorithm B: penicillin blocks bacterial cell wall synthesis, preventing the very accumulation of the avalanche's potential. This represents the first example of the **preventive management of Algorithm B** in a biological system.

### 6.3 Automatic Stock Market Circuit Breakers — Financial Regulators (from 1988)

Following the collapse of the U.S. stock market on October 19, 1987 (Black Monday, −22.6% in a single day), the Brady Commission recommended the introduction of circuit breakers — automatic trading halts upon reaching threshold decline levels [7–9].

The mechanism directly replicates bifurcation point management: when the S&P 500 falls by 7%, trading is halted for 15 minutes; at 13%, a second pause is triggered; at 20%, the market closes for the day. During the COVID-19-related market collapse in March 2020, circuit breakers were triggered four times over two weeks; in three of the four instances, the pause stabilized the decline at the trigger level.

In the terms of the model: the circuit breaker artificially raises α at the moment the avalanche is intensifying — introducing a forced period of Algorithm A action (informational equalization) — allowing participants time for rational reassessment of their positions. However, China's experience in 2016 demonstrated the opposite effect: circuit breakers lasted only four days before being suspended, as the very proximity to the threshold was itself triggering panic selling. This confirms the non-trivial nature of bifurcation management: an incorrectly chosen threshold may **accelerate** the avalanche rather than contain it.

### 6.4 Nuclear Reactors — Controlled Chain Reaction

Nuclear fission is an avalanche process in the purest sense: each decaying atom emits neutrons that trigger the decay of subsequent atoms (Algorithm B in exponential mode). In an atomic bomb, the avalanche unfolds uncontrollably. In a nuclear reactor, control rods made of neutron-absorbing materials (cadmium, boron) serve as regulators of α: they continuously balance the system near criticality, preventing Algorithm B from either dying out (subcritical regime) or exploding (supercritical regime).

This is the most precise physical realization of the concept of a managed duet of two algorithms: the system is intentionally held at α ≈ 0.5 to maximize energy output while maintaining complete control over the avalanche [9].

### 6.5 General Principles of Bifurcation Point Management

The analysis of the above examples reveals three strategies for managing the bifurcation point:

| Strategy | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Raising the threshold | Strengthening Algorithm A, reducing trigger sensitivity | Dynamite, antibiotics |
| Forced pause | Temporarily arresting Algorithm B at the moment of intensification | Stock market circuit breakers |
| Balancing near criticality | Continuous regulation of α to maintain a controlled avalanche | Nuclear reactor |

All three strategies do not eliminate the avalanche per se — they manage its **timing, scale, and direction**. This confirms the central thesis: the avalanche is inevitable once Algorithm B's potential is accumulated; the task of management lies not in its prevention, but in its direction.

## 7. Conditional Intention as a Directing Operator

### 7.1 The Problem of Undirected Desire

A simple desire ("I want X") provides the governing system with a destination point without information about the vector of attainment. In the terms of dynamical systems: the goal is defined, but the landscape is unmarked. Algorithm B, upon an avalanche transition, distributes changes across all available directions, the majority of which do not lead to X.

### 7.2 The IF→THEN Operator as a Cascade Map

A conditional intention of the form:

**"If [condition A] → then [action B] → which opens [condition C] → ..."**

provides the system with a complete map of cascading deployment. Each link in the chain simultaneously constitutes a collapse point of the superposition at the preceding level and a trigger for the next.

Example: *"If revenue grows by a multiple → we lease the adjacent premises → open a second production stream → hire a manager → scale the model to another city."*

The governing system receives not a destination, but a **marked route with intermediate verification points**. This is fundamentally distinct from simple goal-setting.

### 7.3 The Detonating Cord Analogy

Alfred Nobel, in creating a safe detonator, solved the same problem: how to ensure the directed transmission of explosive energy along a prescribed route, while precluding accidental detonation. The Bickford fuse is the physical analogue of conditional intention: the time to detonation is measured in meters of cord, and the cascade is transmitted strictly along the prescribed path.

Conditional intention is the informational equivalent of a detonating cord: it does not prevent the avalanche (which is inevitable given the accumulated potential of Algorithm B), but **determines its direction and the sequence of its deployment**.

### 7.4 Volitional Focus as an Accumulation Mechanism

Sustained attention directed at a conditional chain functions as an amplification of Algorithm A's signal in a specified direction. This creates anisotropy in the potential landscape: when the avalanche arrives at the bifurcation, it is more likely to develop along the pre-established route.

This is consistent with neuroscientific data on directed priming: the prior activation of neural pathways lowers the threshold for their subsequent firing [11].

### 7.5 The Metaphysical Dimension

In the metaphysical dimension, conditional intention is described by a number of traditions under various names: *intention* in Buddhist practice, *kavvanah* in Jewish mysticism, *niyat* in Islamic ethics, and "clear intention" in contemporary cognitive practices. All of these traditions converge on one point: a diffuse desire is less effective than a structured conditional intention.

The proposed model provides a mechanistic explanation for this observation: a structured conditional intention creates a directing vector for the inevitable avalanche, whereas a diffuse desire leaves the direction of the avalanche indeterminate.

## 8. Resolving the Determinism–Indeterminism Debate

Throughout the twentieth century, physics harbored a fundamental tension between the deterministic worldview (Newton, Einstein: "God does not play dice") and the probabilistic interpretation of quantum mechanics (Bohr, Heisenberg: fundamental randomness).

The dual-algorithm model proposes a synthesis:

**The world is simultaneously deterministic and stochastic — not in contradiction, but in conjunction.**

- Algorithm A realizes determinism: within the current stable configuration, the evolution of the system is predictable.
- Algorithm B realizes stochasticity: the accumulation of potential and the moment of the avalanche trigger obey probabilistic laws.
- The interaction of the two algorithms produces the observed picture: predictability within periods of stability, and unpredictability of the *moment* (but not the *fact*) of the avalanche.

Einstein was right: within the domain of Algorithm A, "God does not play dice." Bohr was right: within the domain of Algorithm B, fundamental probability is real. The dispute was a meta-level misunderstanding: both were describing different components of a single mechanism.

## 9. Limitations and Open Questions

The proposed model is conceptual in nature and requires further formalization:

1. **Quantitative specification of Algorithm B:** the potential growth function B(t) is postulated as monotonically increasing, but the precise form of the function for various classes of systems requires empirical determination.

2. **Measurement of the coefficient α:** the operational definition of α for systems of different kinds (physical, biological, social) remains an open question.

3. **The mechanism of avalanche directionality:** precisely how conditional intention creates anisotropy in the potential landscape is a question that requires experimental verification in the cognitive neurosciences.

4. **Relationship to quantum entanglement:** the role of non-local correlations in the mechanism of interaction between Algorithms A and B is not addressed in this paper.

## 10. Conclusion

This paper proposes a dual-algorithm model of nonlinear dynamics that unifies a broad class of threshold-avalanche transitions in physical, biological, economic, and cognitive systems. The model:

1. Eliminates "randomness" as an autonomous explanatory principle, replacing it with the mechanism of Algorithm B's potential accumulation.
2. Generalizes Nobel Prize-winning discoveries in quantum physics, thermodynamics, and the physics of complex systems as particular manifestations of a single mechanism.
3. Introduces the conditional intention operator (IF→THEN) as the informational analogue of a detonating cord — a mechanism for directing the inevitable avalanche along a prescribed route.
4. Resolves the determinism–indeterminism debate through conjunction rather than opposition.

The author invites researchers to critically examine and empirically verify the proposed concepts.
